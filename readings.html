Session 2

📄 "Going Under" — Sarah C. P. Williams, Stanford Medicine magazine (2014)

  • Time / difficulty: ~15 min · ●○○
  • Why you're reading this: Every anesthetic depends on a question no monitor fully answers: is this patient unconscious, and how deeply? This feature follows Stanford anesthesiologists who read raw brain waves (EEG) instead of trusting the single 0–100 "depth" number most operating-room monitors display. It picks up right where Session 1 left off: a device that boils one patient's brain down to one number, and clinicians deciding when to trust it. It's from 2014, so read it for the questions it raises, not as the latest science.
  • Read for this question: The monitor gives one number for "how deep is this patient?" When would you trust that number for the patient in front of you, and what would you check it against?
  • Find it: Stanford Medicine magazine

🎬 "How an Anesthesiologist Sets Up an Operating Room for Surgery" — Max Feinstein, MD (video)

  • Time / difficulty: ~10 min · ●○○
  • Why you're watching this: Before the patient rolls in, the anesthesiologist builds their workspace: the anesthesia machine, the monitors, the airway equipment, and the medications. Watching an anesthesiologist set up an operating room shows how many devices one person depends on, and how much of the job is preparing for things to go wrong. Watch it alongside "Going Under" and notice which machines measure the patient and which ones act on the patient. Our first Session 2 talk, on the monitors and sensors that keep patients safe while they sleep, starts from here.
  • Watch for this question: Pick one machine or monitor in the setup. What does it measure or do, and how would the anesthesiologist know if it were wrong?
  • Find it: YouTube
  • Optional, if you enjoyed it: here's a more technical video on setting up an operating room for cardiac anesthesia: YouTube

🔩 Robotic and radiation-free spine surgery — Stanford Neurosurgery (page + video)

  • Time / difficulty: ~10 min · ●●○
  • Why you're reading this: The second half of Session 2 moves to the other side of the drape with Corinna Zygourakis, MD, a Stanford robotic spine surgeon. This short Stanford Neurosurgery page explains how robots and 3D navigation help surgeons place screws millimeters from the spinal cord, and notes that Dr. Zygourakis was the first neurospine surgeon in the Greater Bay Area to use 7D Surgical, an image-guided system that avoids X-ray radiation. The video shows this kind of navigation in action. Come with questions for her.
  • Read for this question: The page says the robot can "see what the human eye cannot." If the robot's 3D map and the surgeon's own eyes disagree mid-surgery, who should win, and what evidence would convince you either way?
  • Find it: Stanford Neurosurgery page · 7D Surgical video (YouTube)

Session 3

📄 "Real-world data: a brief review of the methods, applications, challenges and opportunities" — Liu & Panagiotakos, BMC Medical Research Methodology (2022)

  • Time / difficulty: ~20 min · ●●○ — read the abstract, "Characteristics, types and applications of RWD," and "Challenges and opportunities." Skim the rest.
  • Why you're reading this: In Session 1 we saw how trials test technology on carefully chosen groups of people. Most health data isn't collected that way. It comes from health records, insurance claims, disease registries, and the watch on your wrist. This primer explains what "real-world data" is, why researchers and regulators want it, and why it is messy, incomplete, and easy to misread. It's the raw material behind the question Session 3's design workshop will argue over: who should own it?
  • Read for this question: Your smartwatch data could help researchers learn about thousands of people. What could make it misleading about a whole population, and what could make it misleading about you?
  • Find it: BMC Medical Research Methodology (open access)

📄 "Privacy protections to encourage use of health-relevant digital data in a learning health system" — McGraw & Mandl, npj Digital Medicine (2021)

  • Time / difficulty: ~15 min · ●●○ — read the abstract, Box 1 (overview), and Box 2 (the four categories of health-relevant data). Keep going if you're hooked.
  • Why you're reading this: Most people assume HIPAA protects all of their health data. It doesn't: HIPAA depends on who holds the data, not what kind of data it is. This paper maps four categories of "health-relevant" data, from hospital records to search histories and credit scores, and argues that clicking "I agree" on a consent form can't protect you on its own. It also argues that health data should still be used to improve care. That tension, protecting data versus putting it to use, is the heart of Session 3.
  • Read for this question: Pick one app or device you use. Which of the paper's four categories of data does it collect, and who, if anyone, protects that data?
  • Find it: npj Digital Medicine (open access)

📄 "A Delphi consensus statement for digital surgery" — Lam et al., npj Digital Medicine (2022)

  • Time / difficulty: ~20 min · ●●● — read the abstract, Textbox 1 (the definition), and the sections on privacy and public trust, consent, and litigation and liability.
  • Why you're reading this: This paper bridges Session 2's operating room and Session 3's data debate. Thirty-eight international experts in surgery, AI, industry, law, ethics, and policy went through four rounds of voting to agree on what "digital surgery" means and which ethical problems come with it. Their conclusions are worth arguing about: data ownership is legally unclear, operating-room video could be used against surgeons in court, and patients should consent separately before companies see their data. It's also a lesson in method: a "Delphi" study is how experts build consensus when evidence is thin.
  • Read for this question: Operating-room video can help train surgical AI, but surgeons worry it could be used against them in court. Who should own the recording of your surgery: you, the surgeon, the hospital, or the company that built the camera?
  • Find it: npj Digital Medicine (open access, CC BY 4.0)
